Content that actually ranks.

Most businesses publish content that sits there doing nothing. Here's what content marketing for local SEO actually looks like when every piece has a job — and everything connects.

M
Michael Beets
Founder & Director, Beets & Co

There's a version of content marketing that's everywhere right now. A business posts a blog article. They share it on Instagram. They move on. The blog doesn't rank. The Instagram post gets 14 likes. Nothing connects to anything else, and the whole exercise quietly disappears.

I see it constantly — businesses investing time (or money) into content that has no real job. No keyword target. No platform purpose. No link back to something that builds authority over time. Content for the sake of looking active.

The businesses that actually get traction from content do the opposite. Every piece they publish serves a specific function — and everything connects back to a central piece of work that earns authority in search.

I want to show you exactly what that looks like, using a real client as the example.

Every piece of content should have a specific job. If you can't name it, the content probably isn't doing one.

The Crowncon case study

Crowncon Homes is a boutique custom home builder operating across South East Melbourne and the Mornington Peninsula. They're not trying to compete with volume builders — they build bespoke homes for clients who want Tim Swindon, the director, personally managing their project from first conversation to handover.

Their most recent project — the Tasman build in Rye — is a 360m² custom home overlooking Bass Strait. Platinum finishes, reverse living design to maximise the views, a detached outbuilding, Sonos whole-home audio, imported Spanish tiles, and a brief built on decades of client trust. Delivered in seven months.

It's exactly the kind of project that should be generating enquiries. And it is — because every piece of content we built around it has a specific job.

Piece one: the blog post

The Tasman blog post targets a specific search: custom home builder Rye. That's not an accident — it's the term a prospective client types when they have a block in Rye and they're looking for a builder who knows the area.

The post is written as a case study — not a service page, not a brochure. It tells the story of the build: the site challenges (a one-metre rear cut, engineered retaining walls, a coastal block with significant grade), the brief (platinum finishes, reverse living, Bass Strait views), and what the clients said when they walked back through the door six months later.

That specificity is the point. Google's guidance around content quality now centres on something called "Information Gain" — rewarding content that adds genuine new information, first-hand experience, or unique perspective. A case study written by the builder who was on site every day, about a real project on a real street, does that. A generic "what to look for in a custom home builder" post does not.

Piece two: the video

This is the part most businesses skip — and it's where the real differentiation happens.

The Tasman build has a professionally shot video. Actual footage of the home — the cathedral ceiling with exposed feature rafters, the double-glazed raked window above the commercial sliding door, the stone detailing, the deck overlooking Bass Strait. The clients on camera, in their own words, talking about how the house makes them feel when they arrive.

No AI tool can produce that. No competitor who didn't build that house can replicate it. And when it's embedded directly into the blog post, it does two important things: it keeps readers on the page significantly longer (a strong ranking signal), and it tells Google that the content has real depth behind it — not just words arranged to match a search query.

The Tasman build, Rye — Crowncon Homes. Footage that no competitor can replicate because none of them built it.

The video also lives on YouTube independently — which means it surfaces in video search results and in the "Video" SERP features that now appear on an increasing number of local search queries. One piece of footage. Multiple places it can be found.

Piece three: social

Instagram and Facebook posts from the Tasman project don't exist as standalone content. They exist to do a specific job: drive traffic back to the blog post and the video.

A post showing the deck view links to the full case study. A Reel using footage from the build links to the YouTube video. A carousel of the kitchen and stone detailing links back to the project page. Every piece of social content points somewhere — it's not a dead end.

This matters for two reasons. First, social traffic to a blog post is a behavioural signal — people arriving, reading, staying on the page. Second, it extends the reach of content that took real effort to produce. A single build generates six months of posts if you approach it with a plan.

Social content that doesn't point anywhere is content that disappears. Every post should have a destination.

Why real content wins

There's a lot of noise right now about AI content. Whether it ranks, whether Google can detect it, whether businesses should be using it to scale their output.

Here's what the data actually shows: human-written content is eight times more likely to rank number one on Google than AI-generated content. Not because Google penalises AI — it doesn't, officially — but because AI content, at scale, tends to look like everything else. It lacks first-hand experience. It lacks specificity. It lacks the thing that makes a reader stay on the page: the feeling that the person who wrote it was actually there.

Tim Swindon was on that Rye site every day. The clients he quotes in that blog post have known him since he was 17. The footage in that video was shot inside a home he built from the ground up on a challenging coastal block, in seven months, with platinum finishes, without compromise. That's not replicable. That's the content that ranks.

The compound effect

Here's what the content ecosystem looks like when all three pieces are in place and connected:

01

The blog post earns organic search traffic

Someone searching custom home builder Rye finds the Tasman case study. They read about a 360m² build delivered in seven months by a builder who was personally on site every day. They watch the video. They book a call.

02

The video surfaces in YouTube and video search

Someone who didn't find the blog finds the YouTube video instead. They watch it. They click through to the website. The video's watch time tells Google the content is worth surfacing again.

03

Social drives signals back to both

Instagram and Facebook posts from the build generate traffic to the blog and views on the video. That traffic signals engagement to Google. Engagement strengthens rankings. Rankings generate more organic traffic. The loop runs itself.

04

AI search tools cite it

When someone asks an AI search tool "who are the best custom home builders in Rye?", the answer is increasingly drawn from content that has depth, specificity, and authority — exactly what this ecosystem provides. Crowncon Homes' AI search health sits at 100%. That's not luck.

What this looks like for you

You don't need to produce more content. You need every piece of content you do produce to earn its place.

Start with a single project, a single completed job, or a single topic you can speak about from genuine first-hand experience. Build one piece of long-form content around it — written for a specific search term, with real detail, in your own voice. Add video if you can, even phone footage shot well is better than nothing. Then plan your social content as signposts that point back to it.

That's content marketing for local SEO. One ecosystem, built properly, will outrank a year of posts that lead nowhere.
